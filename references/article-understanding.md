# Understand the article

Read the whole supplied article before choosing illustration locations.
Accept local Markdown/text, pasted text, or a user-supplied URL. For PDF or
HTML, extract the actual article rather than navigation or unrelated content.
If only an excerpt is available, state that boundary. Do not pretend to have
read unavailable sections.

## Make an objective content map

Record:

1. **Main thesis:** what the author argues or explains, in one or two sentences.
2. **Core concepts:** usually 5–15 for a long article, fewer for a short one.
   Keep meaningful phrases, not vague one-word labels.
3. **Relationships:** prerequisite, containment, transformation, condition,
   independent alternative, or quantitative dependence.
4. **Implicit prerequisites:** what the article assumes rather than explains.
5. **Explanation layers:** foundational concepts, mechanism, consequences,
   exceptions. This is a content map, not an assessment of the reader.
6. **Confusion points:** distinctions that prose alone makes easy to miss.
7. **Evidence ledger:** exact values, units, formulas, conditions and the
   section/table/paragraph they came from.

Then decompose mechanisms:

- Actors/roles: who owns, provides or receives something?
- Phases: which operations share a context?
- Steps/states: what changes, and in what order?
- Connections: what does each directed edge mean?
- Inputs/outputs: what enters or leaves a step?

Use N/A where appropriate. A mathematical function does not need fictional
actors; a list of independent options does not imply a sequence.
Separate the author's claims, arithmetic derived from them, and your own
interpretation. Ask about ambiguous facts instead of inventing relationships.

## Select illustration opportunities

Look for branching decisions, easily confused dimensions, conversions,
comparisons, dependencies and turning points. A picture must explain a
relationship through position, length, boundary or connection, not merely
repackage prose into cards.

For each candidate, record:

```text
Article location:
Reader's question:
One conclusion:
Evidence:
Why geometry helps:
Facts shown:
Details retained in prose/caption:
```

Reject redundant candidates. A short definition may need no diagram.
Do not assume every article requires an overview or a fixed number of images.

## Boundaries

Do not run a knowledge quiz, assign learning hours, invent OKRs, or assess
the reader's personal gaps for an ordinary illustration task. Those belong
to a separately requested learning-plan task.
Never put "you already know this" or unsupported difficulty scores inside
an objective article diagram.

## Explain content choices

Present a short account of the article and the chosen illustration locations.
Explain why each included fact supports the conclusion, and why omitted
detail remains in the surrounding prose. If the source contradicts itself,
stop at that point and ask rather than resolving it silently in the image.
